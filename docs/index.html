Abstract

Which pages should a content team review first, out of thousands, because their search impressions are likely to fall over the next 30 days? We used 78.8 million page-days of Google Search Console data from pseudonymized FlyRank client sites (January 2025 to June 2026), cut into 12 monthly training snapshots, one validation month and one sealed test month. A gradient-boosting model built on 18 features computed from the past only ranked each page by its risk of falling to half or less of its own site's trend; it was validated forward in time with a 30-day embargo and compared with three simple rules and shuffled-label controls. On the sealed test month (75,873 pages, 22.1% of which fell) the model's ranking scored an average-precision lift of about 1.5 against 1.09 for the best simple rule, and 13 to 14 of its top 20 pages fell, about three times the base rate. This is decision-support for where to look first: the edge varies by month and by page volume, and nothing here shows why a page falls or that any action would help.

Read this first (30 seconds)

  • The obvious rules failed on the test month. Ranking by the biggest recent fall did no better than a random queue at the top of the list (lift of 0.7 to 0.9 for the top 20).
  • The model ranked better in every period, but its edge shrank. Average-precision lift was 2.28 over six training months, 1.84 in validation and about 1.5 in the sealed test, as the share of pages that fell rose from about 8% to 22%.
  • It leans on how a page behaves, not on whether it fell last month. Its top picks are often low-volume, intermittently visible pages where counts are noisy, so a person has to check each page before acting.

1. The problem: which pages first?

Picture a content strategist with an hour to spare. At the end of June 2026 the data held 77,251 eligible pages across 22 sites. She can open 20 to 50 of them. Which ones?

The obvious answer is the pages that fell most last month. We tested that, and it is a weak rule. Pages that fell at least a third relative to their site two months in a row were only about 1.4 times as likely to fall again (14.1%, against 10.2% overall). Pages that rose the most were also more likely to fall back (14.8% in the top decile).

This paper builds a ranked review queue instead, and asks three questions. Does it beat the simple rules on months it has never seen? Is one site carrying the result? And where does it stop working?

Which pages are scored, and why others are not

  • Published and not deleted, at least 90 days old. Deleted or unpublished pages carried almost no traffic (170 pages with 39,073 impressions in March 2026, against 176,568 pages and 280.6 million impressions for the rest).
  • At least 100 impressions in the 30 days before the snapshot. Below that, a 20% swing is ordinary noise. About 43% of active pages in March fell below the floor and are not scored.
  • Sites with at least 200 such pages. A site benchmark built from fewer pages is unstable. This was first set at 100 and raised to 200 after one site at the 100-page minimum showed a 92% fall rate; it removed 0.9% of rows.
  • Not new or spiking. Pages whose last 30 days were at least twice both earlier months made up 18.7% of rows but 30.8% of the falls (a 19.8% fall rate against 10.1% for other pages). A spike that settles back is not a page needing attention, so these are tagged "recent spike" and left out of the model.

3. Method

What counts as a fall (the label)

A page counts as falling if, in the 30 days after the snapshot date, its impressions fall to half or less of what its own site's eligible pages did, and both 15-day halves of that window are below its baseline relative to the site. Measuring against the site removes seasonality and tracking changes that move every page together. The 0.5 cut was set from base rates on training months (7% to 20% across three snapshots), never from model performance.

What the model sees

Eighteen features, all built from data up to the snapshot date: volume and click-through over 90 days, impression-weighted position, momentum over consecutive 30-day windows (also relative to the site), how many days the page was visible, how volatile its daily impressions were, and its age. Static content fields (word count, backlinks and similar) are an ablation only, because which ones are missing identifies the site.

How it is tested

  • Forward in time. Each evaluation month is scored by a model trained only on earlier months, dropping any training month whose 30-day label window would run past the evaluation date.
  • Fixed in advance. The model type, settings, page universe and metrics were fixed before the validation and test months were scored. The success rule was written down first: the model's average-precision lift must beat both the best simple rule and the shuffled-label controls.
  • Compared with. Three transparent rules (biggest relative fall, biggest raw fall, most impressions lost), a logistic regression, and a null model trained on labels shuffled within each month (three seeds).
  • Metrics. Precision at the top 20 and top 50 (a reviewer opens 20 to 50 pages), and average precision. Lift divides each by that month's base rate, so 1.0 means no better than random and months with different base rates can be compared.

Leakage checks

No label column or FlyRank product flag is in the feature list. The features for one snapshot were rebuilt from a warehouse cut at the snapshot date, so no later row was read: all 64,669 stored rows matched, with zero differing values across the 18 features. The shuffled-label models stayed between 0.84 and 1.18 in AP lift in every period, far below the real model.

Is one site carrying the result?

A harder check scored each group of sites with a model trained only on the other sites. Over the last four training months the average-precision lift was 2.13, against 2.23 for the main run, so the model does not depend on knowing which site a page belongs to.

The obvious rule's blind spot

Where it is weaker

The top picks come from the low-volume tail, where counts are noisy. Among pages with at least 1,000 impressions the model still led in validation (top-20 lift 4.9 against 1.7) and in the test (3.5 against 2.3), with an overall AP lift of 1.84 against 1.66 in the test. In the last four training months, however, the "most impressions lost" rule matched or beat it at the very top for these larger pages.

5. Limitations and honest framing

  • Observed associations, not causes. No experiment was run. Nothing here shows why a page falls, that a refresh recovers traffic, or how Google ranks pages.
  • One sealed month. The lift fell from 2.3 to 1.8 to about 1.5 while the base rate rose from about 8% to 22%. Part of that is a ceiling on lift at high base rates; one test month cannot separate it from a real decline in skill.
  • The top of the list is noisy. One page is 5 points of precision, and two runs of the same model on the test month gave 14 hits and 13 (most likely small numerical differences between machines). The exact order of the final queue also shifts between refits (one large page ranked 27th in one fit and 59th in another, of 73,363).
  • A few sites dominate. Sites with 2,000 or more eligible pages hold roughly nine in ten training rows. The model beat the rules in 20 of 21 test sites, but small sites have noisy benchmarks. In the final queue, 30 of the top 50 pages come from two sites (seven sites in all).
  • The label is a design choice. It measures a page against its own site, not an absolute drop; it needs 100 impressions to score a page; and spiking pages are out of scope. The site minimum was changed once, after seeing a result.
  • Refresh history is mostly invisible. The last-optimized date keeps one value per page and starts in April 2026, so the data cannot show what refreshing does. Static content fields describe a later export, not the snapshot date.
  • Reason codes describe a page; they are not causes and not exact model attributions. A few high-risk pages meet none of them.

6. Ranked recommendations

These are for a person deciding where to look first. They come from the queue built at 2026-06-30 (77,251 eligible pages, 22 sites). The top 5% of scored pages, about 3,670, are flagged for review. Risk scores rank pages; they are not probabilities.

  1. Review the high-volume picks first. They put the most impressions at stake. Two examples from the queue: a page with 27,894 impressions in the last 30 days against 109,002 before (-74%), and one with 123,819 against 160,975 (-23%). Check first whether the fall is real.
  2. Do not refresh pages that are already rising. Of the 20 highest-risk pages with 1,000 or more impressions, 8 were growing. Past rises tend to give ground back, so their action is "monitor: recent rise may fade".
  3. Decide merge, prune or improve for the low-volume, intermittent, deep-position pages. They fill the top of the overall list (34 of the top 50). Counts are small, so look at each page's last 90 days before acting.
  4. Rule out site-wide events before blaming a page. Seasonality, an outage or a tracking change moves every page on a site.
  5. Leave spiking pages alone. 3,888 pages are tagged "recent spike" and not scored; expect a pullback and monitor them.
  6. Re-score each month and watch for drift. Retrain and re-check if the share of pages that fall moves outside half to double its recent median, if AP lift stays under 1.3 for two months, or if lift at the top 50 stays under 2.0.

7. Reproducibility

Everything is in the GitHub repository. The notebooks, in the order they were built:

  • capstone: data, method, results, limitations and the queue.
  • w01 and w02: the question and the task.
  • w03 data contract and feature and leakage check.
  • w04 signal audit and baseline score.
  • w05 model, w06 validation audit and w07 action playbook.

Seeds and settings. Main model: gradient boosting with 200 iterations, learning rate 0.05, 15 leaves, at least 200 samples per leaf, L2 regularization 1.0, seed 0. Null models use seeds 0 to 2; the random-split comparison uses seed 0. Re-running. The warehouse is gated on Hugging Face and requires accepting the data terms. Run the capstone first (it builds the saved monthly snapshots), then the other notebooks. Expect tiny numerical differences between machines.
